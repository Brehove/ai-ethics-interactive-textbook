# Reading Record Prompt Design

Status: controlling instructor-facing guidance for student reflection checkpoints

Applies to: every PHIL 123 chapter that includes a downloadable reading record

## Purpose

The reading record makes students' thinking visible while they read. It is not a comprehension quiz, an automated tutor, a discussion board, or a substitute for the chapter. Its purpose is to give students a small number of well-chosen occasions to perform philosophical reasoning and preserve evidence of how their judgment developed. Most chapters will still use a Commit–Work–Reconcile sequence, but the instructor may use fewer or more checkpoints when the chapter's intellectual work warrants it.

Every prompt should help a student do at least one of the following:

- articulate a provisional judgment;
- explain a philosophical claim or distinction;
- reconstruct an inference;
- identify an assumption or warrant;
- test a principle against another case;
- generate a counterexample or serious alternative;
- formulate a question the text has made possible;
- calibrate confidence under uncertainty;
- identify why a judgment changed, strengthened, or remained stable.

The downloaded record is submitted through Canvas as evidence of engaged reading. The website does not grade, evaluate, transmit, or persist the response.

## Controlling Design Commitments

1. **Checkpoint count is an instructor judgment, not a schema limit.** A chapter may contain zero, one, or many checkpoints. Keep interruptions scarce enough that the chapter remains a reading experience; add each checkpoint only when its anchored passage enables a distinct, worthwhile reasoning move.
2. **The original response is preserved.** A committed response cannot be silently overwritten. Later checkpoints provide space to qualify or revise earlier thinking.
3. **Prompts have contextual passages and explicit positions.** Each checkpoint names the stable passage whose intellectual work makes the prompt timely, while its `checkpointRef` position in ordered chapter flow determines where it appears. Context never overrides visible flow order.
4. **Prompts are disciplinary.** Students should practice philosophical interpretation, argumentation, judgment, objection, or conceptual analysis rather than generic self-expression.
5. **Prompts are constructive.** A student must produce an explanation, inference, distinction, question, case, or judgment that the text did not simply state verbatim.
6. **Prompts are open but constrained.** More than one defensible answer should be possible, but the task and criteria for a serious response must be clear.
7. **Prompts do not reveal a preferred verdict.** Guidance may name the reasoning operation, not the conclusion students are expected to reach.
8. **The chapter remains complete without JavaScript.** Checkpoints enhance the reading but never contain required chapter content.
9. **Student writing stays ephemeral.** No response database, account, analytics event, browser storage, or network transmission is permitted.
10. **The prompt set tracks development.** When a chapter uses multiple checkpoints, the responses should form an intelligible sequence rather than unrelated journal entries.

### Public reader presentation

The public first-read route is content-first. The chapter occupies the default reading surface, while the reading-record response and notes panel remains closed until the reader explicitly opens it from an inline checkpoint or header control. A closed panel must not reserve horizontal space or leave the prose aligned as though the panel were present. Opening the panel may temporarily create a two-surface workspace; closing it returns the reader to the centered chapter without clearing page-memory responses.

This presentation rule does not remove or duplicate checkpoint data. Inline checkpoint cards and the response panel continue to render from the same checkpoint record, and the editor continues to preview both forms. Required chapter content must never depend on opening the optional panel.

## Recommended Commit–Work–Reconcile Pattern

This is the recommended default, not a publication constraint. The specific reasoning strategy and checkpoint count may vary with the chapter. An instructor may omit a stage, repeat a stage with a descriptive `slotLabel`, or add a checkpoint for another genuine reasoning hinge.

### 1. Commit: establish a starting position

Place this checkpoint after the opening dilemma, orienting example, or first framing of the chapter's problem and before the chapter has supplied its complete philosophical vocabulary.

The student should preserve one of the following:

- an initial ethical judgment;
- a provisional definition;
- a prediction about what a principle would imply;
- an assumption the student brings to the issue;
- an initial confidence level and reason.

The prompt must require a reason. A preference without justification is not yet a philosophical judgment.

Example:

> At this point, do you judge the AI-written apology to be assistance, substitution, or something in between? What feature of the case matters most to your judgment?

### 2. Work: perform the chapter's central reasoning move

Place this checkpoint immediately after the passage where the chapter's central concept, inference, distinction, or problem becomes available for use.

Select one primary strategy from the repertoire below. Do not combine three or four operations into a miniature essay assignment. The student should be able to produce a serious response in roughly two to five sentences.

Examples of appropriate chapter-specific work:

- reconstruct the bridge from habituation to character;
- distinguish cleverness from practical wisdom;
- expose a hidden premise in a Kantian argument;
- test a utilitarian principle with a contrast case;
- identify what evidence would support a claim about algorithmic bias;
- formulate the strongest question left unresolved by an existential-risk argument.

### 3. Reconcile: form a considered judgment

Place this checkpoint after the most serious objection, comparison, complication, or limit in the chapter, but before references and supplementary reading.

The student identifies whether the opening judgment was:

- changed;
- qualified;
- strengthened; or
- left substantially unchanged.

The response must also identify:

1. the claim, distinction, example, or objection that caused or failed to cause movement;
2. the strongest reason now supporting the judgment; and
3. the most serious objection or uncertainty that remains.

Unchanged is a legitimate result. The student must explain why the reading failed to dislodge the initial judgment rather than treating immobility as self-justifying.

## Strategy Repertoire

Use these identifiers in prompt planning notes and future structured prompt records.

### `self-explanation`

**Reasoning move:** Integrate a difficult claim with prior knowledge by explaining how or why it works.

**Useful when:** A passage introduces a technical concept, distinction, mechanism, or sequence.

**Prompt pattern:**

> Explain how the author gets from [idea A] to [idea B]. Which step is least obvious, and why?

**Avoid:** "Summarize this section." Summary can be copied without explaining the inferential relationship.

### `argument-reconstruction`

**Reasoning move:** Identify the conclusion, stated reasons, and an unstated premise needed for the inference.

**Useful when:** The passage contains an argument central to the chapter.

**Prompt pattern:**

> State the conclusion in your own words. What must also be true for the stated reason to support it?

**Avoid:** Asking students merely to label P1, P2, and C when the labels have already been supplied.

### `evidence-warrant`

**Reasoning move:** Distinguish evidence from the warrant connecting it to a claim and evaluate the strength of support.

**Useful when:** A philosopher, institution, case study, dataset, or historical example is used to justify a broader conclusion.

**Prompt pattern:**

> The author uses [evidence or case] to support [claim]. What is the unstated bridge between them? Does it support the strong claim or only a weaker one?

**Avoid:** Treating a citation or example as self-interpreting proof.

### `contrast-case`

**Reasoning move:** Change one feature of a case to identify which feature controls the judgment.

**Useful when:** A dilemma contains several morally relevant facts or the chapter stresses sensitivity to particulars.

**Prompt pattern:**

> Change one feature of this case that would reverse your judgment. Why does that feature do the ethical work?

**Avoid:** Changing so many facts that the student is analyzing an unrelated case.

### `counterexample`

**Reasoning move:** Test the scope of a proposed principle by generating an apparently unacceptable implication.

**Useful when:** The reading states or relies on a general rule, criterion, or definition.

**Prompt pattern:**

> Construct a case in which this principle produces an implausible verdict. Should the principle be rejected, restricted, or defended?

**Avoid:** Requesting a counterexample when the relevant claim is not universal or when the chapter has not yet stated it precisely enough to test.

### `consider-alternative`

**Reasoning move:** Generate a genuinely plausible explanation, interpretation, or judgment that conflicts with the student's current view.

**Useful when:** Belief preservation, polarized positions, or an apparently obvious verdict threatens inquiry.

**Prompt pattern:**

> Give the strongest plausible interpretation of this case that conflicts with your current view. What makes it plausible?

**Avoid:** "Give the other side." The alternative must be specific, plausible, and responsive to the same evidence.

### `objection-repair`

**Reasoning move:** State a serious objection and determine whether it defeats, limits, or requires revision of an argument.

**Useful when:** The chapter has supplied enough of the framework for students to pressure-test it.

**Prompt pattern:**

> State the objection in a form its defender would recognize. Does it defeat the argument, narrow its conclusion, or require a new premise?

**Avoid:** Soft objections selected only because they are easy to dismiss.

### `question-generation`

**Reasoning move:** Identify a genuine ambiguity, tension, missing justification, or unresolved implication and formulate the question that exposes it.

**Useful when:** The chapter should cultivate inquiry rather than settle a dilemma.

**Prompt pattern:**

> What is the most important question this passage has not answered? Explain what makes the question philosophically difficult and what a satisfactory answer would need to address.

**Avoid:** "What questions do you have?" It gives no criterion for distinguishing a productive question from a request for factual clarification.

### `epistemic-calibration`

**Reasoning move:** Relate confidence to the quality and limits of available justification.

**Useful when:** The case is ill-structured, evidence is incomplete, or reasonable disagreement remains.

**Prompt pattern:**

> How confident are you in this judgment? What evidence or argument would genuinely raise or lower that confidence?

**Avoid:** Confidence ratings without an explanation of what would produce rational movement.

### `framework-comparison`

**Reasoning move:** Compare frameworks by the questions they ask, grounds they invoke, and considerations they make salient.

**Useful when:** A chapter explicitly places traditions or theories in conversation.

**Prompt pattern:**

> These frameworks reach similar verdicts. What different reason does each treat as decisive, and what does one notice that the other may miss?

**Avoid:** Comparing frameworks only by whether their final verdicts agree.

### `transfer`

**Reasoning move:** Apply a concept or argument to a structurally related case and identify the limits of the analogy.

**Useful when:** Students should demonstrate understanding beyond the chapter's worked example.

**Prompt pattern:**

> Apply this principle to a different AI-mediated practice. What remains structurally the same, and what ethically relevant feature changes?

**Avoid:** Near-copy cases that only require replacing one noun.

### `metacognitive-trace`

**Reasoning move:** Identify the source and character of a change in thinking.

**Useful when:** Always available to the final checkpoint, but it should be combined with substantive philosophical justification.

**Prompt pattern:**

> Which claim, distinction, example, or objection most affected your judgment? What reasoning move did it cause you to make?

**Avoid:** "What did you learn?" or "How did this reading make you feel?"

## Anatomy of a Checkpoint

Every checkpoint plan must specify the following fields.

| Field | Function | Requirement |
| --- | --- | --- |
| `id` | Stable identity | Chapter-scoped, descriptive, and unchanged after publication unless the checkpoint is intentionally replaced |
| `passageId` | Intellectual context | Existing stable passage ID whose work makes the checkpoint timely; it does not determine inline position in schema v3 |
| `flowPosition` | Visible placement | Explicit position of the checkpoint reference before or after an existing flow node |
| `stage` | Position in the sequence | `Opening judgment`, chapter-specific hinge label, or `Exit judgment` |
| `strategy` | Intellectual operation | One primary identifier from the repertoire |
| `title` | Panel heading | Short imperative or task description |
| `trigger` | Invitation inside the reading | A concise question that signals why this is a useful place to pause |
| `prompt` | Student task | One clear, answerable philosophical operation |
| `guidance` | Quality criterion | Explains what a serious response should accomplish without supplying its conclusion |
| `responseStructure` | Input design | `prose`, `movement-plus-prose`, or another approved non-evaluative structure |
| `rationale` | Instructor/agent note | Why this passage and strategy serve the chapter's learning work |

The student-facing trigger, prompt, and guidance should use plain language. Strategy identifiers and design rationale remain instructor-facing metadata.

## Required Agent Workflow

An agent designing or revising checkpoints must complete these steps in order.

### 1. Read the complete chapter

Do not generate prompts from the chapter title, metadata, learning objectives, or a summary. Identify:

- the opening problem;
- the central philosophical operation;
- the strongest complication or objection;
- the chapter's stable passage IDs;
- any existing exercises that should not be duplicated.

### 2. Write a one-sentence chapter reasoning objective

Use the form:

> By the end of this chapter, students should be better able to [philosophical operation] in order to [kind of judgment or argument].

If the sentence only says "understand," "learn about," or "reflect on," it is not precise enough to guide prompt selection.

### 3. Select the necessary anchors

- If the chapter uses an opening judgment, anchor it before the chapter resolves the dilemma.
- Anchor each working checkpoint immediately after the passage that enables its selected reasoning strategy.
- If the chapter uses an exit judgment, anchor it after serious pressure on the framework and before references or optional further reading.

Do not choose anchors merely to space the prompts evenly.

### 4. Select one primary strategy per checkpoint

The opening normally uses initial judgment or assumption surfacing. The middle uses the chapter's central strategy. The exit normally uses metacognitive tracing plus considered judgment.

Across the book, rotate middle strategies so students repeatedly practice a range of philosophical operations. Do not force every chapter into the Aristotle pattern.

### 5. Draft the student-facing language

Use this order:

1. concise trigger;
2. single principal task;
3. one guidance sentence naming the quality criterion.

Prefer verbs such as `explain`, `reconstruct`, `distinguish`, `identify`, `test`, `compare`, `generate`, `justify`, and `revise`.

Avoid `discuss`, `reflect on`, `share your thoughts`, `what do you think`, and `how do you feel` unless followed by a precise reasoning operation.

### 6. Test the set

Before implementation, the agent must answer:

- Can a student copy a sentence from the chapter and appear to complete the task? If yes, revise it.
- Does the question require knowledge the chapter has not yet supplied? If yes, move or revise it.
- Does the wording imply the instructor's preferred conclusion? If yes, neutralize it.
- Could two thoughtful students reasonably disagree? If no, determine whether the checkpoint has become a quiz.
- Does the guidance explain quality without doing the reasoning? If no, revise it.
- Does the sequence produce a visible intellectual trajectory? If no, redesign the set.
- Is the total response burden proportionate to the chapter and its number of pauses? If no, remove or reduce checkpoints.

### 7. Produce the planning record before code changes

Use this template in the agent's working notes or pull-request description:

```yaml
chapterId: chNN
reasoningObjective: >-
  By the end of this chapter, students should be better able to ...

  - id: opening-judgment
```

Implementation must not begin until the planning record passes the quality gate below.

## Prompt Quality Gate

Each checkpoint must pass every criterion.

| Criterion | Passing question |
| --- | --- |
| Disciplinary specificity | Does the student perform an identifiable philosophical operation? |
| Textual timing | Has the anchored passage made this operation possible and useful now? |
| Constructive engagement | Must the student generate reasoning not already written in the chapter? |
| Justification | Does the task require a reason, warrant, distinction, or evidential basis? |
| Genuine openness | Can more than one answer be responsibly defended? |
| Constraint | Is it clear what a serious answer must accomplish? |
| Neutrality | Does the prompt avoid signaling the expected verdict? |
| Cognitive economy | Can the task be completed seriously without becoming a miniature essay? |
| Sequence | Does the response contribute to a visible before-during-after trajectory? |
| Privacy | Can the interaction run without saving or transmitting student text? |

If any criterion fails, the checkpoint is not ready for implementation.

## Common Failure Modes

Reject prompts that exhibit these patterns:

- **Generic reaction:** "What are your thoughts about Aristotle?"
- **Mood reflection:** "How did this reading make you feel?"
- **Recall disguised as reflection:** "What are Aristotle's four main concepts?"
- **Leading question:** "Why does outsourcing apologies damage your character?"
- **False balance:** "Give both sides" when one side has not been made plausible.
- **Overloaded prompt:** one checkpoint demands summary, argument reconstruction, application, objection, and personal reflection.
- **Premature demand:** the prompt asks students to apply a distinction before the text explains it.
- **Decorative personalization:** the student names a personal experience but does no philosophical work with it.
- **Unanchored confidence:** the student supplies a number without explaining what evidence warrants it or could change it.
- **Performative revision:** the design implies that changing one's mind is better than responsibly retaining a judgment.
- **Automated verdict:** the interface scores the response or tells the student whether the ethical judgment is correct.

## Chapter 7 Worked Example

Reasoning objective:

> By the end of this chapter, students should be better able to evaluate an AI-mediated practice in terms of habituation and practical wisdom, while stating the strongest limit of their own virtue-ethics judgment.

### Opening judgment

- **Anchor:** `ch07-p0004`
- **Strategy:** initial judgment and assumption surfacing
- **Prompt:** At this point, do you judge the AI-written apology to be assistance, substitution, or something in between? What feature of the case matters most to your judgment?
- **Rationale:** The student commits before Aristotle's vocabulary supplies an interpretation.

### Conceptual hinge

- **Anchor:** `ch07-p0041`
- **Strategy:** self-explanation with a contrast case
- **Prompt:** Aristotle distinguishes noticing, deliberating, and choosing. Which of these must remain yours in the apology case? Cite the idea in this passage that supports your answer. Then change one feature of the case under which your answer might be different.
- **Rationale:** The student uses *phronesis* rather than merely defining it and demonstrates sensitivity to particulars.

### Exit judgment

- **Anchor:** `ch07-p0062`
- **Strategy:** metacognitive trace and objection-repair
- **Response structure:** movement choice (`Changed`, `Qualified`, `Strengthened`, or `Unchanged`) plus concise prose
- **Prompts:**
  - The idea that most affected my judgment was...
  - My strongest reason now is...
  - The objection or uncertainty that remains is...
- **Rationale:** The response distinguishes movement from improvement and preserves the strongest remaining pressure on the judgment.

## Research Basis

This design draws on several related research traditions. The findings support the architecture; they do not mechanically determine the wording of any individual PHIL 123 prompt.

### Explicit and embedded critical-thinking instruction

Abrami et al. synthesized 341 effect sizes from experimental and quasi-experimental studies. Critical-thinking instruction had a positive overall effect, with particularly useful contributions from authentic problems, dialogue, and mentoring. Their analysis also adopts the American Philosophical Association's categories of interpretation, analysis, evaluation, inference, explanation, and self-regulation.

- Abrami, Philip C., et al. "Strategies for Teaching Students to Think Critically: A Meta-Analysis." *Review of Educational Research*, vol. 85, no. 2, 2015, pp. 275-314. <https://doi.org/10.3102/0034654314551063>

### Self-explanation and constructive engagement

Prompted self-explanation can improve understanding of expository text by requiring learners to integrate new information with existing knowledge. The ICAP framework classifies activities such as self-explaining, comparing, generating inferences, and asking questions as constructive because students produce something beyond the supplied material.

- Chi, Michelene T. H., et al. "Eliciting Self-Explanations Improves Understanding." *Cognitive Science*, vol. 18, no. 3, 1994, pp. 439-477. <https://doi.org/10.1207/s15516709cog1803_3>
- Chi, Michelene T. H., and Ruth Wylie. "The ICAP Framework: Linking Cognitive Engagement to Active Learning Outcomes." *Educational Psychologist*, vol. 49, no. 4, 2014, pp. 219-243. <https://doi.org/10.1080/00461520.2014.965823>

### Alternatives, counterarguments, and belief preservation

Research on "consider the opposite" and multiple plausible explanations shows that generating a serious alternative can reduce biased assimilation and belief perseverance. Argumentation research also indicates that criteria instruction can improve students' integration of arguments and counterarguments.

- Lord, Charles G., Mark R. Lepper, and Elizabeth Preston. "Considering the Opposite: A Corrective Strategy for Social Judgment." *Journal of Personality and Social Psychology*, vol. 47, no. 6, 1984, pp. 1231-1243. <https://doi.org/10.1037/0022-3514.47.6.1231>
- Hirt, Edward R., and Keith D. Markman. "Multiple Explanation: A Consider-an-Alternative Strategy for Debiasing Judgments." *Journal of Personality and Social Psychology*, vol. 69, no. 6, 1995, pp. 1069-1086. <https://doi.org/10.1037/0022-3514.69.6.1069>
- Nussbaum, E. Michael, and Gregory Schraw. "Promoting Argument-Counterargument Integration in Students' Writing." *The Journal of Experimental Education*, vol. 76, no. 1, 2007, pp. 59-92. <https://doi.org/10.3200/JEXE.76.1.59-92>

### Reflective judgment and metacognition

Reflective-judgment research treats mature judgment as the coordination of claims, evidence, and standards of justification under uncertainty. Metacognitive instruction makes planning, monitoring, and evaluation explicit rather than assuming students will spontaneously inspect their own thinking.

- Kitchener, Karen Strohm, and Patricia M. King. "Reflective Judgment: Concepts of Justification and Their Relationship to Age and Education." *Journal of Applied Developmental Psychology*, vol. 2, no. 2, 1981, pp. 89-116. <https://doi.org/10.1016/0193-3973(81)90032-0>
- Kuhn, Deanna. "A Developmental Model of Critical Thinking." *Educational Researcher*, vol. 28, no. 2, 1999, pp. 16-25. <https://doi.org/10.3102/0013189X028002016>
- Tanner, Kimberly D. "Promoting Student Metacognition." *CBE-Life Sciences Education*, vol. 11, no. 2, 2012, pp. 113-120. <https://doi.org/10.1187/cbe.12-03-0033>

### Student-generated questions

Teaching students to generate questions about a text improves comprehension on new material. The research also warns that prompts can over-scaffold: if the system supplies too much of the intellectual organization, students do not develop their own question structures. Some PHIL 123 checkpoints should therefore ask students to formulate the question rather than always answer one supplied by the textbook.

- Rosenshine, Barak, Carla Meister, and Saul Chapman. "Teaching Students to Generate Questions: A Review of the Intervention Studies." *Review of Educational Research*, vol. 66, no. 2, 1996, pp. 181-221. <https://doi.org/10.3102/00346543066002181>
- King, Alison. "Guiding Knowledge Construction in the Classroom: Effects of Teaching Children How to Question and How to Explain." *American Educational Research Journal*, vol. 31, no. 2, 1994, pp. 338-368. <https://doi.org/10.3102/00028312031002338>

## Evidence Boundary

Confidence is high in the broad principles: explicit disciplinary reasoning, authentic dilemmas, constructive generation, serious alternatives, argument-counterargument integration, metacognitive monitoring, and student question generation.

Confidence is moderate in the exact distribution, number, and wording of prompts across PHIL 123. Much of the evidence crosses age groups and disciplines, and no published study establishes one universally correct checkpoint count for an introductory college AI-ethics textbook. Treat each chapter's prompt sequence as a research-informed design hypothesis that should be evaluated through student work, instructor observation, accessibility testing, and revision.

Do not infer that:

- more prompting is always better;
- changing one's judgment is evidence of learning;
- written fluency is identical to philosophical understanding;
- a confidence rating is meaningful without reasons;
- an AI-generated follow-up question is pedagogically sound without instructor review;
- completion of a reading record proves that the student read every passage.

## Maintenance

Revise this guide when:

- classroom evidence shows a recurring prompt failure;
- a new interaction structure changes the flexible-checkpoint contract;
- the research basis materially changes;
- a strategy identifier is added, removed, or redefined.

Record substantial changes in the pull-request description. Do not silently change the meaning of an existing strategy identifier after chapter prompts depend on it.
