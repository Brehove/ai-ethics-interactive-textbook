# Is It Good Because God Commands It? Divine Command, Natural Law, and Moral Authority

<!-- phil-section-id: ch05-s001 -->
## A Rule Can Bind Without Explaining Itself

<!-- phil-passage-id: ch05-p0001 -->
Imagine a student who is behind in the semester and considering something the syllabus explicitly forbids. The assignment is a short written response, and the instructions say the response must be completed without AI assistance. She has been using a chatbot all week for other classes. It would take her ninety seconds to paste the prompt, adjust the wording, and submit. Her friends do it. Her instructor probably will not catch it. She still hesitates.

<!-- phil-passage-id: ch05-p0002 -->
If you ask her why she hesitates, she will give you several answers, and they will not all be the same kind of answer. She might say she hesitates because the instructor said not to. She might say she hesitates because God forbids deception. She might say she hesitates because it feels like cheating, and cheating damages the trust her classmates and instructor have in her work. She might say ghostwriting a response would form a habit she does not want. She might say the assignment is there for a reason, and skipping the thinking undermines the reason. She might say the likely harms of getting caught, or of not learning the material, outweigh the ninety seconds saved.

<!-- phil-passage-id: ch05-p0003 -->
Each of those answers is doing something different underneath the surface. The first answer treats the syllabus as the source of the obligation. The second treats God as the source. The third points to trust and truthfulness. The fourth points to character. The fifth points to the goods the assignment is meant to protect. The sixth weighs consequences. All six answers might reach the same conclusion. All six answers rely on very different accounts of what makes the conclusion morally binding.

<!-- phil-passage-id: ch05-p0004 -->
This chapter is about that difference. When somebody tells you an action is wrong, they are usually appealing to something. They might appeal to a command, a rule, a scripture, a policy, a shared human nature, a person's dignity, a virtuous character, the good of a community, or the balance of consequences. What philosophers call the *ground* of a moral claim is whatever the claim is finally leaning on. Two people can reach the same judgment while leaning on very different grounds. Two people can lean on the same ground and still reach different judgments about the specific case.

<!-- phil-passage-id: ch05-p0005 -->
Being under authority is not the same as understanding moral authority. Being under authority means somebody is telling you what to do. Understanding moral authority means knowing why that instruction has any moral weight at all. The student who hesitates in front of her assignment already knows the syllabus forbids what she is considering. The philosophical question is deeper. What makes the syllabus, or God, or human nature, or her own conscience, count as a source of obligation in the first place?

<!-- phil-passage-id: ch05-p0006 -->
Religious ethics provides a direct version of this question because religious traditions have long examined what it means to be commanded by a being higher than yourself. If God tells you not to lie, is lying wrong because God said so? Or does God tell you not to lie because lying is already wrong, and God, being good, would only ever command what is good? Those two possibilities sound close. They pull moral reasoning in very different directions.

<!-- phil-passage-id: ch05-p0007 -->
The rest of this chapter walks through two answers religious ethics has developed. The first grounds moral obligation in God's command. The second, developed by [Thomas Aquinas](/people/thomas-aquinas/), grounds moral reasoning in a rational order of human goods that God's law both establishes and clarifies. The chapter ends by showing how the difference between grounding and method lets you use the other frameworks you will encounter in this course, virtue ethics, Kantian ethics, and consequentialism, without collapsing them into a generic "ethics" that treats every appeal the same way.

## Socrates Asks What Euthyphro Thinks He Already Knows

<!-- phil-passage-id: ch05-p0008 -->
[Plato](/people/plato/)'s dialogue [`Euthyphro`](https://www.gutenberg.org/files/1642/1642-h/1642-h.htm) is set outside an Athenian courthouse. Socrates has been summoned to face charges of impiety and corrupting the youth. He is waiting for the preliminary hearing that will lead, eventually, to his trial and death. Outside the same courthouse he runs into a younger man named Euthyphro. Euthyphro has come to prosecute a case of his own. He is charging his own father with murder.

<!-- phil-passage-id: ch05-p0009 -->
The murder charge is complicated. A worker on Euthyphro's family estate had killed one of the household slaves in a drunken rage. Euthyphro's father bound the worker, threw him into a ditch, and sent someone to ask what to do. Before the messenger returned, the bound man died of exposure and hunger. Euthyphro thinks his father is responsible for that death, and he thinks piety requires him to prosecute his own father for it. The family disagrees. They believe going to court against one's own father is itself impious. Euthyphro is confident they are wrong. He knows what piety is. He is prepared to act on that knowledge in a case that most people would find morally horrifying.

<!-- phil-passage-id: ch05-p0010 -->
Socrates, being Socrates, decides this is the perfect moment to ask a question. If Euthyphro knows piety so well that he can prosecute his own father on the strength of it, then Euthyphro is exactly the person who can tell Socrates what piety actually is. Socrates is about to be tried for impiety. He would like to know what he has been charged with.

<!-- phil-passage-id: ch05-p0011 -->
Euthyphro tries to answer. His first attempt is to point to what he himself is doing. Piety is prosecuting the wrongdoer, whoever the wrongdoer is, even one's own father. Socrates distinguishes an example of pious action from a definition of what makes any action pious. A definition would tell you what all pious actions have in common. Without that, you cannot decide whether a new case counts.

<!-- phil-passage-id: ch05-p0012 -->
Euthyphro tries again. Piety is what is loved by the gods. Impiety is what is hated by them. Socrates presses on this. The Greek gods notoriously disagree with each other. The Iliad is largely a record of the gods taking opposite sides in every fight. If the gods disagree about a given action, then the same action would be pious and impious at the same time. Euthyphro sees the problem and tightens his answer. Piety is what all the gods love. Impiety is what all the gods hate.

<!-- phil-passage-id: ch05-p0013 -->
Now Socrates asks the question that later philosophers will adapt into a problem about divine command. In Benjamin Jowett's translation, he asks whether the holy is "[beloved by the gods because it is holy, or holy because it is beloved of the gods](https://www.gutenberg.org/files/1642/1642-h/1642-h.htm)." Plato's immediate subject is piety and the Greek gods' love. The familiar divine-command dilemma is a later monotheistic reformulation. Both versions separate two directions of explanation: divine approval may recognize a feature the action already has, or divine approval may create that feature.

<!-- phil-passage-id: ch05-p0014 -->
At first the two options sound like the same thing said twice. They are two very different accounts of piety. If the pious is loved by the gods because it is pious, then piety is a real feature of certain actions, and the gods recognize which actions have it. Divine approval responds to something already present in the action. If the pious is pious because it is loved by the gods, then divine approval is what makes it pious under Euthyphro's definition.

<!-- phil-passage-id: ch05-p0015 -->
The [Internet Encyclopedia of Philosophy entry on divine command theory](https://iep.utm.edu/divine-command-theory/) treats this question as the standard pressure test for any view that grounds ethics in divine will. The dilemma works because both answers cost something. If the gods love the pious because it is already pious, then piety has a standard that runs alongside or beneath the gods. That threatens the idea that the divine is the source of morality. If the pious is pious because the gods love it, then morality looks arbitrary. The gods might have loved something else. They might have loved cruelty. If they had, cruelty would have been pious.

<!-- phil-passage-id: ch05-p0016 -->
<aside class="textbox shaded textbox--key-point" role="note" aria-labelledby="euthyphro-two-horns">
<h3 id="euthyphro-two-horns">Key Point: The Later Divine-Command Reformulation</h3>
<ol>
<li><strong>Command recognizes moral status:</strong> God commands an action because it is already required, or forbids it because it is already wrong.</li>
<li><strong>Command constitutes obligation:</strong> the action is required or forbidden because God commands or forbids it.</li>
</ol>
<p>The pressure comes from what each option seems to cost. The first can make obligation look independent of command. The second can make obligation look arbitrary. Divine command theory centrally concerns what makes actions obligatory or forbidden; it need not claim that every kind of value is created by a command.</p>
</aside>

<!-- phil-passage-id: ch05-p0017 -->
Euthyphro cannot hold his position under this pressure. He tries to shift ground several more times and each attempt loses more of what he began with. By the end of the dialogue he is late for his hearing and eager to leave. Socrates lets him go without settling the question. The dialogue is designed to leave you feeling the vulnerability of any moral position that leans on approval without being able to say what the approval is tracking. Piety, as a concept, is left open. The pressure test on divine authority is what you carry with you.

<!-- phil-passage-id: ch05-p0018 -->
You can carry an analogy to the Euthyphro test out of the courthouse and into ordinary moral reasoning. Whenever somebody appeals to an authority to settle a moral question, ask what gives that authority moral weight. A syllabus can establish a course requirement without settling the moral question by itself. If the syllabus forbids ghostwriting, the instructor may be protecting truthfulness, fair evaluation, or the student's opportunity to learn. Those goods explain the rule's moral purpose. The Euthyphro dilemma concerns divine authority, so a syllabus is not another instance of the same theological problem. The analogy helps you distinguish compliance with a rule from a reasoned account of why the rule deserves obedience.

<!-- phil-section-id: ch05-s003 -->
## When Command Creates Obligation

<!-- phil-passage-id: ch05-p0019 -->
Divine command reasoning takes one side of the later Euthyphro reformulation seriously. It grounds moral obligation in God's command. On this view, an action is morally required because God requires it, and an action is morally forbidden because God forbids it. If God forbids deception, then the believer is obligated not to deceive because the believer is answerable to God.

<!-- phil-passage-id: ch05-p0020 -->
Divine command theory is one command-based form within the broader family of theological voluntarism. The [Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy entry on theological voluntarism](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/voluntarism-theological/) uses "voluntarism" because the family centers on God's will, from the Latin *voluntas*. Not every version requires an explicit spoken command. Some ground moral obligation in God's will whether or not that will has been verbally expressed. The shared claim is that moral obligation stands under God's will.

<!-- phil-passage-id: ch05-p0021 -->
<aside class="textbox shaded textbox--caution" role="note" aria-labelledby="dct-beyond-rule-following">
<h3 id="dct-beyond-rule-following">Caution: Divine Command Goes Beyond Rule-Following</h3>
<p>Divine command reasoning grounds obligation in God's command or will. Generic rule-following appeals to policy, custom, enforcement, or institutional authority. A syllabus rule may bind a student inside a course. Divine command theory makes a stronger claim about divine authority as the ground of moral obligation.</p>
</aside>

<!-- phil-passage-id: ch05-p0022 -->
Divine command reasoning has serious philosophical attractions. It gives moral obligation a personal and ultimate source: the believer is answerable to God. Within many theistic traditions, prayer, worship, scripture, interpretation, and moral obedience belong to a shared religious life. Students who place God at the center of moral life may recognize this account immediately. Other students can still reconstruct and test the argument without claiming a belief they do not hold.

<!-- phil-passage-id: ch05-p0023 -->
The view also produces a clean way to begin an argument. A particular tradition may read a scriptural command, teaching, or established interpretive source as prohibiting deception. The reasoner must then show why the action in the case belongs under that prohibition. Applied to the ghostwriting case, the argument begins with a command against deception and an account of why presenting generated work as unaided work counts as deception. The later argument section will make that interpretive bridge visible.

<!-- phil-passage-id: ch05-p0024 -->
The argument's simplicity is part of its appeal. Identify what God forbids, then show that the action falls under the prohibition. Consequences, virtues, and universal maxims do not supply the decisive inference in this framework.

<!-- phil-passage-id: ch05-p0025 -->
The Euthyphro question sits directly on top of this simplicity. If deception is forbidden because God forbids it, then what is it about God's forbidding that creates the obligation? The original dilemma presents two pressures, but these do not exhaust later divine-command responses. This chapter follows those pressures into Aquinas's natural-law account rather than surveying every later version of DCT.

<!-- phil-passage-id: ch05-p0026 -->
The first answer says God's command is the whole story. Deception is wrong because God forbids it, and that is the beginning and end of the account. On that answer, deception has no further feature that God is tracking. God could, in principle, have commanded deception, and if God had, deception would have been morally required. This makes morality appear arbitrary. It also makes it hard to say what people mean when they call God good. If good simply means "commanded by God," then saying God is good means only that God commands what God commands, which is not saying very much.

<!-- phil-passage-id: ch05-p0027 -->
The second answer says God commands what is good because God recognizes what is good. Deception is wrong for reasons that make sense apart from the command, and God, being wise and loving, forbids it because it is wrong. This answer preserves the sense that God's commands track something real. It also weakens the claim that God's command is the ground of moral obligation. If deception is already wrong, then the command is a wise instruction pointing at the wrongness, and the wrongness itself is grounded somewhere else.

<!-- phil-passage-id: ch05-p0028 -->
A further problem shows up wherever divine command reasoning has to be applied. Even people who agree God has commanded something have to interpret the command. Scripture is read in different ways within a single tradition and across traditions. Communities have their own histories of reading. Individuals have their own consciences. The command "do not deceive" is easy to state and much harder to apply, because deception has to be defined, and different traditions have defined it differently. Divine command reasoning does not, by itself, tell you which reading is the correct one. It hands the interpretive work off to something else.

<!-- phil-passage-id: ch05-p0029 -->
None of this is a refutation of religious ethics. It is a live problem inside religious ethics, and religious ethics has been thinking about it for centuries. Aquinas developed one of the most influential Western answers. He keeps God as the source of moral order while changing what "source" means.

<!-- phil-section-id: ch05-s004 -->
## Aquinas Changes the Question

<!-- phil-passage-id: ch05-p0030 -->
Thomas Aquinas was a thirteenth-century Dominican friar and theologian whose long unfinished work, the [`Summa Theologiae`](https://www.newadvent.org/summa/2090.htm), became one of the central sources for Western natural-law thinking. When Aquinas gets to the questions on law inside the *Summa*, he does not begin by asking what God commands. He begins by asking what law itself is.

<!-- phil-passage-id: ch05-p0031 -->
His answer, worked out in [Question 90](https://www.newadvent.org/summa/2090.htm), is that law belongs to reason. Aquinas's compact definition calls law "an ordinance of reason for the common good." The full definition adds two more conditions: law comes from someone responsible for the community and must be promulgated, or made knowable to those it governs. Aquinas is arguing that any genuine law, including divine law, has this rational structure. A ruler may have the power to enforce an arbitrary command, but coercive power alone does not give the command the fullest moral character of law.

<!-- phil-passage-id: ch05-p0032 -->
Aquinas treats God's law as reason ordering creatures toward their good. God's commands are lawful because they arise from wisdom and aim at the good of those governed. The [Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy entry on Aquinas](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/aquinas-moral-political/) explains that law addresses mind, choice, and love rather than operating as physical necessity or coercive power alone.

<!-- phil-passage-id: ch05-p0033 -->
A Thomistic reply to the Euthyphro pressure can be reconstructed from these claims. Aquinas holds that [God is essentially and supremely good](https://www.newadvent.org/summa/1006.htm), that creation reflects divine reason, and that human nature is directed toward goods reason can partly recognize. God's law therefore expresses the same rational order that directs human beings toward those goods. The good does not stand above God as an independent standard, and a divine command does not create obligation by arbitrary force. This reply still depends on Aquinas's theological account of why created human nature and its ends are good. Someone who rejects that account will not be compelled by the grounding, even if the structure is now visible.

<!-- phil-passage-id: ch05-p0034 -->
Aquinas's four kinds of law, worked out in [Question 91](https://www.newadvent.org/summa/2091.htm), show how this rational order appears at different levels of creation. Reading the distinctions slowly keeps their different functions visible.

<!-- phil-passage-id: ch05-p0035 -->
Eternal law is Aquinas's name for divine wisdom directing created things toward their ends. Fire heats. Trees grow toward light. Bees form hives. Human beings pursue understanding. In Aquinas's language, everything is measured by the divine reason from which it comes.

<!-- phil-passage-id: ch05-p0036 -->
Natural law is Aquinas's name for how eternal law shows up in rational creatures. Human beings, because they are rational, do not only follow eternal law the way water fills a container or fire heats what it touches. They participate in it. They can grasp, at least partially, why certain actions serve human goods and certain actions damage them. Aquinas says natural law is the participation of the rational creature in the eternal law. You do not need to be told everything you need to know about how to live. You have a kind of built-in orientation toward goods, and reason lets you work out what those goods are and how to act on them.

<!-- phil-passage-id: ch05-p0037 -->
Human law is Aquinas's name for positive law made by a responsible political or public authority for the common good. Local ordinances and national statutes are straightforward examples. Aquinas argues that human law derives specific rules from more general natural-law principles and that an unjust law has a qualified, diminished claim on conscience ([Question 96](https://www.newadvent.org/summa/2096.htm)). A university syllabus is a smaller institutional rule, not automatically human law in Aquinas's political and juridical sense. His framework still gives us criteria for evaluating it by analogy. Does the rule come from a legitimate authority within the course? Was it communicated clearly? Does it reasonably order the course toward shared educational goods? Those questions explain why a syllabus rule may deserve more than compliance based on fear of punishment.

<!-- phil-passage-id: ch05-p0038 -->
Divine law, in Aquinas's account, is law given by direct revelation. Scripture is the paradigm case. It directs human beings toward an ultimate end that unaided reason cannot fully know and clarifies parts of the moral order that reason gets wrong or misses. Divine and natural law express the same eternal order through revelation and practical reason.

<!-- phil-passage-id: ch05-p0039 -->
<aside class="textbox shaded textbox--framework-map" role="note" aria-labelledby="aquinas-four-laws">
<h3 id="aquinas-four-laws">Framework Map: Aquinas's Four Kinds of Law</h3>
<dl>
<dt><strong>Eternal law</strong></dt>
<dd>God's rational ordering of creation toward its proper ends.</dd>
<dt><strong>Natural law</strong></dt>
<dd>Human participation in eternal law through reason and practical judgment.</dd>
<dt><strong>Human law</strong></dt>
<dd>Positive law enacted by political or public authority for the common good.</dd>
<dt><strong>Divine law</strong></dt>
<dd>Revealed law that directs human beings toward their ultimate end and clarifies what reason may miss.</dd>
</dl>
</aside>

<!-- phil-passage-id: ch05-p0040 -->
Aquinas's four laws provide several routes through the ghostwriting case. Ask why submitting AI-generated work as your own is wrong. A divine-command argument begins from a command against deception, but it still needs an interpretation showing why this practice belongs under the command.

<!-- phil-passage-id: ch05-p0041 -->
On Aquinas's view, the answer can run through several channels at once. Knowledge and social life are among the goods practical reason recognizes; truthful representation and genuine learning help realize them. The syllabus gives a specific institutional rule for this assignment. Its moral force depends partly on whether the rule reasonably protects the goods the course is meant to develop. Aquinas's account of human law helps us test that rule by analogy without turning every classroom policy into civil law. Scripture, for a student who treats it as authoritative, may confirm the prohibition against deception and connect truthfulness with a person's ultimate end. That is divine law. Within Aquinas's system, rightly ordered forms of law participate in the same eternal order while doing different jobs.

<!-- phil-passage-id: ch05-p0042 -->
Two common misunderstandings obscure natural law. The first treats whatever is biologically common as morally right. Aquinas instead argues that reason can recognize goods such as life, knowledge, social life, and rational conduct because human beings are oriented toward them. Reason must distinguish the goods flourishing requires from the many things that merely happen. The [Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy entry on the natural law tradition](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/natural-law-ethics/) frames natural law as a claim about principles of practical rationality rather than statistical averages of animal behavior.

<!-- phil-passage-id: ch05-p0043 -->
<aside class="textbox shaded textbox--caution" role="note" aria-labelledby="natural-law-human-goods">
<h3 id="natural-law-human-goods">Caution: Natural Law Concerns Human Goods</h3>
<p>For Aquinas, natural law begins with practical reason. It asks which actions support goods such as life, knowledge, social life, and rational conduct. Truthfulness and genuine learning are practices and virtues that help realize those goods. Biology supplies examples of living creatures; the moral argument depends on rational goods.</p>
</aside>

<!-- phil-passage-id: ch05-p0044 -->
The second misunderstanding is that natural law collapses into divine command, so all the Euthyphro problems come back through a side door. That reading misses the structure. On Aquinas's view, truthfulness belongs to a rational order of created human nature. The command against deception expresses that order, and reason can partially work out the same conclusion. In [Question 94](https://www.newadvent.org/summa/2094.htm), Aquinas states the first precept of practical reason in compact form: "good is to be done and pursued, and evil is to be avoided." The sentence does not settle a case. It explains where practical reasoning starts. A reasoner still has to identify the goods involved and determine whether a particular action supports or damages them.

<!-- phil-passage-id: ch05-p0045 -->
A Thomistic reply to the Euthyphro pressure therefore runs through one rational order. Deception damages knowledge and truthful social life, goods toward which human beings are rationally oriented. God, whose eternal law establishes that orientation, forbids what damages it. Command and goodness express the same divine reason rather than two independent standards joined by a later choice.

<!-- phil-section-id: ch05-s005 -->
## Can Reason Reliably Identify Human Goods?

<!-- phil-passage-id: ch05-p0046 -->
Natural law now faces its own serious pressure. The theory says practical reason can recognize human goods. How does a reasoner distinguish a genuine good from a strong preference, a familiar custom, or an inherited assumption about what human life should look like? People pursue knowledge and friendship. They also pursue power, prestige, and domination. Human directedness by itself cannot tell us which pursuits perfect human life and which distort it.

<!-- phil-passage-id: ch05-p0047 -->
The [Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy's current account of the natural-law tradition](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/natural-law-ethics/) shows that this difficulty appears inside the tradition. One method emphasizes **derivation**: study human capacities and explain which activities fulfill them. Another emphasizes **inclination**: begin from the ends human beings persistently pursue and reflect on why those ends appear good. The methods can supplement each other, but each needs a bridge. Derivation must explain how a description of human capacities supports a moral conclusion. Inclination must explain why some persistent desires reveal genuine goods while others reveal corruption or confusion.

<!-- phil-passage-id: ch05-p0048 -->
Natural-law theorists also disagree about the catalog of goods. Aquinas reasons from inclinations and precepts concerning life, procreation and education, knowledge, social life, and rational conduct. Later natural-law theorists systematize overlapping but different lists of what they call **basic goods**. Even a settled list would leave another question: how should a person respond when goods conflict, and why does protecting a given good require this action rather than another? The objection therefore reaches three parts of a natural-law argument: the premise identifying a human good, the evidence or reasoning supporting that classification, and the bridge from the good to the proposed action.

<!-- phil-passage-id: ch05-p0049 -->
Aquinas's own account gives the tradition a partial reply. He does not treat every inclination as morally trustworthy; practical reason interprets and directs inclination, while reflection on human inclinations gives reason evidence about the ends people pursue. That reciprocal testing addresses how goods might be known, but it does not eliminate dispute. Aquinas separately distinguishes general principles from their application. In [Question 94, Article 4](https://www.newadvent.org/summa/2094.htm), he says that "the more we descend to matters of detail, the more frequently we encounter defects." Cultures and individuals may derive different rules because practical reasoning remains fallible, especially as the act, its purpose, and its circumstances become more complex.

<!-- phil-passage-id: ch05-p0050 -->
That reply changes how a student should build a natural-law argument. Writing `truthfulness protects knowledge and social life` begins the reasoning. The student still has to explain the connection, show why the action damages those goods, identify complicating circumstances, and limit the conclusion to what the argument supports. A natural-law argument becomes stronger when those bridges are visible enough to challenge and revise.

<!-- phil-section-id: ch05-s006 -->
## Grounding and Method

<!-- phil-passage-id: ch05-p0051 -->
Once you see how natural law works, you can look at the frameworks you will meet later in this course and notice something helpful. Many of them use similar questions. They ground the questions differently.

<!-- phil-passage-id: ch05-p0052 -->
A virtue ethicist, following Aristotle or a modern successor, will ask what kind of person you are becoming. The [Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy entry on virtue ethics](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/ethics-virtue/) organizes the family around the question of character. What habits does this action form? What virtues does it express or damage? What would a person of practical wisdom do here? A Kantian, following [the tradition described in the SEP entry on Kant's moral philosophy](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/kant-moral/), will ask about dignity and rational agency. Are you treating other people as ends, or using them merely as means to your convenience? Can the maxim of your action be willed as a universal law? A consequentialist, along the lines mapped in the [SEP entry on consequentialism](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/consequentialism/), will ask about outcomes. Who is helped, who is harmed, at what scale, with what probability, over what time?

<!-- phil-passage-id: ch05-p0053 -->
An Aquinian natural-law reasoner can ask related questions, but the overlap requires care. Habits matter because virtues help a person perceive and pursue human goods. Respect for persons can arise from the rational and social nature of human beings, although Kant's language of rational agency and treating persons as ends belongs to Kant's own framework. Consequences matter because actions can protect or damage real goods and the common good, although natural law does not reduce judgment to maximizing an aggregate result. A natural-law argument can therefore reach concerns that resemble later frameworks without becoming Kantian, utilitarian, or identical with Aristotle's virtue ethics.

<!-- phil-passage-id: ch05-p0054 -->
The distinction between **method** and **grounding** helps explain the overlap. A method is a question that directs attention to part of a moral situation. Grounding explains why the answer to that question carries moral authority. Asking what habits an action forms is a method. A Thomistic natural-law reasoner grounds the concern in goods and virtues that belong to human flourishing within eternal law. A virtue ethicist who rejects that theological account can ask a similar question while grounding it in an Aristotelian or another non-theological account of flourishing.

<!-- phil-passage-id: ch05-p0055 -->
Frameworks can disagree at both levels. They may disagree about the source of moral authority, which considerations have priority, or which test should govern when character, duties, and consequences point in different directions. Similar vocabulary does not erase those disagreements.

<!-- phil-passage-id: ch05-p0056 -->
Apply this to the ghostwriting case one more time. A virtue ethicist would ask what habit ghostwriting the response would form in this student. Would it strengthen her honesty or corrode it? Would it build the intellectual patience the assignment is meant to develop, or would it teach her to work around difficulty in ways that will cost her later? A Kantian would ask whether she is treating her instructor and classmates as ends in themselves or using them as a mechanism to get a grade. A consequentialist would ask what happens if she does this once, if she does it often, if her classmates find out, if she does not learn what the assignment is designed to teach, and how the theory evaluates the overall consequences across the class and semester.

<!-- phil-passage-id: ch05-p0057 -->
An Aquinian natural-law reasoner can consider all three while translating them into the framework's own terms. Ghostwriting may damage truthfulness and learning, interfere with a truthful relationship between student and instructor, reinforce habits that make those goods harder to pursue, and produce harmful effects within the class. The natural-law reasoner grounds these concerns in a rational order of human goods and asks whether the action responds to those goods without defect. A virtue ethicist may ground the character concern in a different account of flourishing. A Kantian grounds respect in the demands of rational agency. A consequentialist grounds the judgment in the comparative balance of effects.

<!-- phil-passage-id: ch05-p0058 -->
When these frameworks conflict, the dispute concerns both grounding and priority. Some contemporary natural-law theories, especially versions influenced by John Finnis, resist treating basic goods as interchangeable units on one scale. Consequentialist theories evaluate choices through their comparative effects, though their accounts of value, rules, rights, and distribution differ. The frameworks may agree that truthfulness belongs in the analysis while disagreeing about whether it can be traded against other goods and what would justify the trade.

<!-- phil-passage-id: ch05-p0059 -->

## Building and Testing the Argument

<!-- phil-passage-id: ch05-p0062 -->
Chapter 29 introduced `P1/P2/C` as a compact starting form. Three lines can record your present judgment, but the labels do not prove that every needed step is visible. A `P2` often combines case facts with a disputed moral classification. The argument should expand when a real missing step appears.

<!-- phil-passage-id: ch05-p0063 -->
Consider a divine-command route. A student may reconstruct a tradition's reasoning without personally endorsing it, but the student may not invent a divine command. The first premise should identify a real command, teaching, or interpretive source from the assigned chapter or a tradition the student can represent accurately.

<!-- phil-passage-id: ch05-p0064 -->
<aside class="textbox examples textbox--worked-argument" aria-labelledby="worked-argument-divine-command">
<h3 id="worked-argument-divine-command">Worked Argument: The Divine-Command Route</h3>
<p><strong>N1: Framework principle.</strong> If God forbids an action, believers who stand under that command are morally obligated not to do it.</p>
<p><strong>S1: Interpreted source.</strong> Within the tradition I am reconstructing, [identified source or teaching] is interpreted as a divine command against deception.</p>
<p><strong>F1: Case facts.</strong> The assignment requires unaided writing, and the student submitted a generated response without disclosure as if it were unaided work.</p>
<p><strong>B1: Classification bridge.</strong> Presenting a generated response as unaided work under this requirement counts as deception under that interpretation of the command.</p>
<p><strong>C: Scoped conclusion.</strong> Therefore, within this divine-command argument, submitting the response is morally forbidden.</p>
</aside>

<!-- phil-passage-id: ch05-p0065 -->
The brackets are a required research and interpretation step. A finished argument replaces them with the actual source and interpretation being reconstructed. `N1` states the framework principle that connects divine prohibition with obligation. `S1` identifies the interpreted source. `F1` states what happened, and `B1` classifies those facts under the command. A critic can now press the framework principle, source, interpretation, assignment evidence, classification bridge, or conclusion's scope. Someone might accept a command against deception while denying that grammar assistance or disclosed brainstorming falls under it.

<!-- phil-passage-id: ch05-p0066 -->
A natural-law route makes a different ground visible.

<!-- phil-passage-id: ch05-p0067 -->
<aside class="textbox examples textbox--worked-argument" aria-labelledby="worked-argument-natural-law">
<h3 id="worked-argument-natural-law">Worked Argument: The Natural-Law Route</h3>
<p><strong>N1: Framework principle.</strong> A choice is wrong when its act, aim, or circumstances direct the person against human goods that practical reason should protect.</p>
<p><strong>G1: Goods at stake.</strong> Knowledge and social life are human goods; truthful representation and genuine learning help realize them.</p>
<p><strong>F1: Case facts.</strong> The assignment requires unaided writing, and the student submitted a generated response without disclosure as if it were unaided work.</p>
<p><strong>B1: Application bridge.</strong> Replacing the student's thinking while presenting the result as unaided directs the student against those goods by falsifying what was learned and bypassing the independent practice the assignment protects.</p>
<p><strong>C: Scoped conclusion.</strong> Therefore, submitting this generated response is morally wrong.</p>
</aside>

<!-- phil-passage-id: ch05-p0068 -->
The pressure now falls in different places. A critic can question the principle in `N1`, the goods identified in `G1`, whether truthful representation and unaided practice serve those goods here, or whether `B1` reaches too far. The same critic may accept the conclusion for full ghostwriting while rejecting a rule against spellcheck, translation assistance, or feedback on a student-written draft. That nearby case pressures the scope of the bridge and conclusion.

<!-- phil-passage-id: ch05-p0069 -->
Pressure should lead to a controlled response. The student may **defend** the bridge by explaining why the undisclosed substitution prevents truthful evaluation. The student may **revise** it to distinguish replacing the student's reasoning from support applied to student-authored work. The student may **narrow** the conclusion to assignments that explicitly require unaided writing and whose learning purpose depends on independent practice. The student may **concede** that the argument does not prohibit a particular disclosed use.

<!-- phil-passage-id: ch05-p0070 -->
Suppose the original natural-law conclusion said that all AI assistance damages learning. The altered cases show that this conclusion covers more than the argument establishes. A narrower version says that submitting a generated response as unaided work damages truthfulness and bypasses the independent practice this assignment was designed to assess. That repair preserves the judgment about ghostwriting without pretending the argument has settled every use of AI in writing.

<!-- phil-passage-id: ch05-p0071 -->
The divine-command argument requires a parallel repair. A student who identifies a command against deception must still defend the interpretation and classification. If the tradition or source does not clearly address contemporary writing tools, the student should say how the command is being extended and acknowledge the interpretive step. The final conclusion should track that interpretation instead of claiming that every religious believer must accept one technological boundary.

<!-- phil-passage-id: ch05-p0072 -->
<aside class="textbox shaded textbox--argument-template" role="note" aria-labelledby="command-natural-law-template">
<h3 id="command-natural-law-template">Argument Snapshot: Make the Bridge Visible</h3>
<p><strong>Divine command:</strong> N1 states the framework principle. S1 identifies the command and interpretive source. F1 states the case facts. B1 explains why those facts fall under the command. C states the limited obligation.</p>
<p><strong>Natural law:</strong> N1 states how defective response to goods becomes morally relevant. G1 identifies the goods and the practices that realize them. F1 states the case facts. B1 explains the defect in this case. C states the limited judgment.</p>
<p>Begin compactly. Add the bridge when someone could accept the stated facts and standard while still resisting the conclusion.</p>
</aside>

<!-- phil-passage-id: ch05-p0073 -->
When you use a chatbot to help with this argument, keep its contribution visible. A chatbot can propose a bridge, objection, altered case, or repair. Treat that wording as a `Tutor proposal`. Accepting it does not establish that the premise is accurate or that the reasoning is yours. Restate any proposal you keep and run four checks:

<!-- phil-passage-id: ch05-p0074 -->
1. Verify that a cited command or passage exists and has been represented in context.
2. Verify whether a claimed good comes from Aquinas, a later natural-law theorist, or the chatbot's own synthesis.
3. Test the factual premise and the classification bridge separately.
4. Check that the conclusion's scope and decisive inference remain inside the framework being used.

<!-- phil-passage-id: ch05-p0075 -->
Chatbots also smooth different frameworks into a vague appeal to "ethics." A response may combine Aquinas, Kant, and consequentialism because their vocabularies sometimes overlap. Check what grounds the conclusion and which inference each concept supports. The student remains answerable for every premise retained in the final reconstruction.

<!-- phil-passage-id: ch05-p0076 -->
A short practice will help you see this. Consider the following four claims about the ghostwriting case. For each one, identify what the claim is grounding the moral judgment in.

<!-- phil-passage-id: ch05-p0077 -->
1. Submitting AI-generated writing as your own is wrong because your syllabus forbids it.
2. Submitting AI-generated writing as your own is wrong because God forbids deception.
3. Submitting AI-generated writing as your own is wrong because practical reason directs us to protect knowledge and social life, while ghostwriting undermines those goods by misrepresenting what the student learned and produced.
4. Submitting AI-generated writing as your own is wrong because it forms a habit of dishonesty that will corrode the kind of person you are becoming.

<!-- phil-passage-id: ch05-p0078 -->
The first claim grounds the judgment in the authority of a human rule. It still needs a bridge explaining why that rule has moral weight, perhaps because it reasonably protects truthful evaluation and learning. The Euthyphro dilemma is not literally a syllabus problem, though the authority analogy helps expose the missing reason. The second claim grounds obligation in divine command and needs an identified interpretive route. The third uses natural law by connecting the case with a proposed human good. The fourth uses a virtue-ethics method. It could be grounded in a natural-law account or in another account of flourishing.

<!-- phil-passage-id: ch05-p0079 -->
You do not need to memorize every label before this practice becomes useful. In front of any moral claim, ask what the conclusion is leaning on. That question helps you catch chatbot blending, unsupported public arguments, and shifts in your own reasoning.

<!-- phil-section-id: ch05-s008 -->
## What This Framework Clarifies and What It Cannot Settle

<!-- phil-passage-id: ch05-p0080 -->
The chapter began with a student hesitating in front of a small decision that felt bigger than it was. She may have felt answerable to God, seen damage to knowledge and truthful social life, anticipated the habits the choice would form, or weighed its effects on herself and others. The frameworks in this course make those different routes available for careful thought.

<!-- phil-passage-id: ch05-p0081 -->
Divine command reasoning clarifies the personal structure of moral obligation. The believer follows a command because the believer is answerable to God, not merely because a rule appears on a page. The framework also inherits a pressure test that religious ethics has to keep answering. Any purely command-based view has to say what the command tracks, and if the answer is nothing at all, moral reasoning starts to look arbitrary.

<!-- phil-passage-id: ch05-p0082 -->
Aquinas's natural law clarifies a different feature. Moral reasoning often uses reasons that could be shared with somebody who does not accept your religious tradition. When you tell a friend that dishonesty damages trust, you are usually pointing at something you believe your friend can recognize through reason. On Aquinas's view, God establishes the goods in which practical reason participates and divine law directs people toward an ultimate end that reason cannot fully know by itself.

<!-- phil-passage-id: ch05-p0083 -->
What neither framework can settle, on its own, is the run of hard cases that show up in ordinary life. Even if you accept divine command reasoning, you still have to interpret what God has commanded, and that interpretation is where much of the real disagreement lives. Even if you accept natural law, you still have to defend the proposed goods, explain how they guide this case, and decide what to do when they seem to pull against each other. Aquinas's distinction between general principles and uncertain applications leaves room for disagreement and error. It does not supply a mechanical procedure that turns a list of human goods into one unavoidable answer.

<!-- phil-passage-id: ch05-p0084 -->
Philosophical judgment remains necessary because neither framework applies itself. A chapter, chatbot, or checklist may expose a missing premise, but the student must verify the source, defend the bridge, and decide how far the conclusion reaches. Later chapters will test character and practical wisdom, duties and respect for persons, consequences and stakeholders, and relationships of care. The questions sometimes overlap; the grounds and decisive inferences do not.

<!-- phil-passage-id: ch05-p0085 -->
The next chapter looks at authority in a different setting. When institutions and companies write documents that encode values into technical systems, ask whose values are being encoded, what grounds their authority, and how those values are interpreted and revised. The Euthyphro analogy tests whether authority recognizes or creates the relevant standard. Aquinas's account asks whether rules express reason and serve the goods of those governed.

<!-- phil-passage-id: ch05-p0086 -->
When somebody tells you an action is right or wrong, ask what the reason is grounded in. Ask whether the action is wrong because an authority forbids it, because it responds defectively to a good, because it forms a bad character, because of its consequences, or because several arguments converge. Then press the reason the way Socrates pressed Euthyphro. A defensible judgment should make its ground, bridge, and remaining vulnerability visible.
